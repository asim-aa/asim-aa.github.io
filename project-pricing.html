Uncertainty-Aware Marketplace Pricing Analysis

A pricing pipeline over 426,880 Craigslist vehicle listings that doesn't just predict price, it predicts an honest interval, and audits where that interval lies to itself.

RoleSolo: modeling, conformal prediction, reliability auditing
Timeline426,880 listings, 41,746 held out for evaluation
StackPython · pandas · scikit-learn · CatBoost · Conformal Prediction · Tableau · Ruff
Tableau dashboard ↗ Source on GitHub

Problem

A point estimate of a used vehicle's price ("this car is worth $14,200") is much less useful than an honest range, and a range is only trustworthy if you've checked that it actually covers reality across every price segment, not just on average. A model that hits its nominal 90% coverage target overall could still be badly wrong for the vehicles where getting it wrong matters most.

Approach & architecture

Started with 426,880 raw Craigslist vehicle listings and built 21 engineered features, with a chronological split and VIN-based leakage controls so the same vehicle never leaks across train/test. I compared four modeling approaches in order of increasing complexity: a global median baseline, hierarchical segment medians, Ridge regression, and CatBoost. to make sure the final model's lift was real and not just noise.

CatBoost won on raw error, but a point prediction alone doesn't tell a user how much to trust it. So I layered split conformal prediction on top to generate calibrated interval estimates, then ran subgroup reliability audits to check whether those intervals actually held up across price segments.

Key engineering decisions

VIN-based leakage control was non-negotiable: used-vehicle marketplaces often have the same car relisted multiple times, and without deduplicating by VIN, a model can "cheat" by memorizing a listing it already saw in training. Chronological splitting (not random) matched how the pipeline would actually be used: predicting forward, not interpolating backward.

What broke, and what it revealed

The headline number, 87.88% coverage against a nominal 90% target, looked close enough to reasonable. The subgroup audit told a sharper story: severe undercoverage on $50K+ vehicles, exactly the segment where a wrong interval is most expensive to a buyer or seller. Averaging across all price ranges had hidden a real, actionable gap.

Process: baselines before complexity

Before trusting CatBoost's error number, I compared it against two simpler approaches of increasing complexity: a single global median price, and a hierarchical segment median keyed on manufacturer, model, and age. The segment median set the strongest stable baseline; a Ridge regression was also tried but produced an extreme prediction on a rare feature combination, a reminder that an aggregate metric alone can hide a real failure case.

Process: where coverage actually breaks down

Split conformal prediction turns calibration residuals into empirical intervals at any nominal level; the 90% interval is the headline number, but checking all three nominal levels against their actual test coverage shows the same undercoverage pattern holding across the board, worse in the later, more recent slice of the chronological test period.

What I'd take into the next project

An interval's nominal coverage rate is a starting point, not a conclusion: the same discipline of slicing by a variable that matters operationally (here, price segment; on the NBA project, rest days) is what turns a plausible-looking model into one you can actually trust in the situations that count.

NBA Win-Probability Prediction Platform

An 11-season pregame win-probability pipeline that doesn't just predict who wins, it tells you how much to trust that prediction, and where it breaks down.

RoleSolo: modeling, feature engineering, serving
Timeline11 NBA seasons of historical data, 8 walk-forward cutoffs
StackPython · scikit-learn · CatBoost · FastAPI · Streamlit · SQLite · pytest · GitHub Actions
Source on GitHub

Problem

Most win-probability models optimize a single number, accuracy or AUC on a held-out test set, and stop there. That number can look excellent while hiding a model that's badly miscalibrated in specific, predictable situations, like when a team is playing the second half of a back-to-back. I wanted to build something that would surface that gap instead of averaging it away.

Approach & architecture

The pipeline pulls 11 seasons of NBA game logs and builds a chronological train/test split: no shuffling, no leakage from future games into past predictions. A custom Elo engine tracks team strength over time, updated game by game. On top of that sits a leakage-safe roster-continuity feature: instead of using injury reports directly (which aren't always available historically), it proxies player availability from who actually took the court in recent games, which turned out to be a stronger and more honest signal.

I evaluated logistic regression against CatBoost and picked logistic regression: it matched CatBoost on discrimination while being easier to calibrate and audit, which mattered more than squeezing out marginal AUC.

Key engineering decisions

A walk-forward backtest across 8 season cutoffs stood in for real deployment: retrain on everything before a cutoff, evaluate only on what comes after, repeat. That's what caught the calibration gap, a single train/test split wouldn't have. I also built a live calibration monitor into the serving layer so the gap between predicted and actual win rates is visible continuously, not just at training time.

What broke, and what it revealed

The aggregate numbers (0.738 ROC-AUC, 67.8% accuracy) looked fine on their own. Splitting by rest situation told a different story: a 1.7× calibration-error gap between back-to-back games and rested games. The model wasn't wrong about who was more likely to win; it was overconfident specifically in the situations where fatigue matters most. That's the kind of failure a single summary metric is built to hide.

Process: the one-time test rule

The project spec draws a hard line: the held-out test set gets read exactly once, after the modeling approach is frozen on validation performance alone. When I added the roster-continuity feature after the initial freeze (v1.1), I validated it the same way, on validation data only, before touching test, then re-ran the one-time evaluation as a new, documented iteration rather than reopening the original result.

Where the calibration gap really lives

The headline 1.7× calibration gap is one number from one test cutoff. Two supplementary diagnostics go further without reopening the frozen result: a walk-forward backtest across 8 season cutoffs, and a calibration breakdown by rest status.

What I'd take into the next project

A model's average performance and its worst-case performance are different questions, and you only find the second one by deliberately slicing the data along axes that matter operationally (rest, home/away, injury context), not just axes that are easy to compute. That habit carried directly into the conformal-prediction work on the marketplace pricing project.
